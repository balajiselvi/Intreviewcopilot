Q21. Explain SAP_ALL and SAP_NEW. What is the
difference?
Interviewer's Objective
This is one of the most common questions asked to determine whether you understand
SAP authorization concepts beyond basic administration.
Executive Answer
Both SAP_ALL and SAP_NEW are SAP standard authorization profiles, but they serve
completely different purposes.
SAP_ALL
SAP_ALL grants almost unrestricted access to the SAP system.
It contains nearly every authorization object with unrestricted values.
A user assigned SAP_ALL can:
Execute almost every transaction
Maintain configuration
Create users
Modify roles
Debug programs
Maintain tables
Transport changes
SAP_ALL should never be assigned permanently to business users.
Chapter 3 – Advanced Authorization Concepts

Typical use cases:
Emergency troubleshooting
Initial system setup
Temporary administrator access
Sandbox systems
SAP_NEW
SAP_NEW is completely different.
It is designed to provide temporary access to new authorization checks introduced after
upgrades.
Example:
ECC Enhancement Pack
↓
S/4HANA Upgrade
↓
SAP introduces new authorization objects
↓
SAP_NEW temporarily grants those missing checks.
Its purpose is to avoid business disruption immediately after upgrades.
Key Differences
SAP_ALLSAP_NEW
Full system accessTemporary upgrade support
Administrative profileCompatibility profile
Used sparinglyUsed during upgrades
Security risk if misusedHelps avoid authorization failures

Best Practice
Never leave SAP_NEW assigned permanently after an upgrade.
Once role redesign is complete and authorization objects are incorporated into business
roles, SAP_NEW should be removed.

Real-World Scenario
Interviewer
"An auditor reports that a user accessed a sensitive table through SE16N. How would you
investigate and prevent recurrence?"
Executive Answer
My investigation would follow a structured approach:
1. Confirm which table was accessed and identify its sensitivity.
2. Review the user's assigned roles in SU01 and PFCG.
3. Analyze authorization objects, particularly S_TABU_DIS and S_TABU_NAM, to
determine why access was permitted.
4. Check whether debugging or other elevated privileges contributed to the access.

5. Review audit logs and STAUTHTRACE, if available, to understand the execution
path.
6. Identify whether the access was legitimate, accidental, or unauthorized.
7. If excessive access exists, redesign the role based on least privilege, transport the
changes through the landscape, and document the remediation for audit purposes.
8. Recommend periodic reviews of table access and additional monitoring for high-risk
tables.
This approach demonstrates technical competence, governance awareness, and audit
readiness.
